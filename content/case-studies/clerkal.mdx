## 1 — Overview

**Clerkal** is clinical documentation software for dentists and doctors.
Instead of typing the same clinical note after every appointment, the
clinician clicks through a template — options, toggles, dropdowns sitting
inline in real sentences — dictates the parts that vary, and Clerkal
assembles a clean plain-text note they paste into their practice's
patient management system.

I built the editor at the centre of it: a **Slate.js** document that
behaves like a form and serialises like prose.

## 2 — The challenge

A clinician finishes an appointment and has to write it up. The note is
not creative writing — it is the same skeleton every single time.
Presenting complaint, medical history, social history, dental history,
extraoral exam, treatment, plan. Ninety percent of the words are
identical to the last patient's. The ten percent that differ are the
part that matters clinically, and they're the part that gets rushed,
because the clinician is typing the other ninety percent by hand while
the next patient is already in the waiting room.

Two obvious solutions both fail. A **plain rich text editor** means
retyping the skeleton all day. A **form** means the output stops being a
note — and the note has to leave the app as plain text, because it gets
pasted into whatever patient management system the practice runs, and
there are hundreds of those.

The constraints that shaped everything after this:

- **The output is plain text, not our format.** It has to survive being
  pasted into a twenty-year-old Windows EHR with no stray characters.
- **No install.** It runs in the browser next to 200+ healthcare
  systems, because practices can't add software to clinical machines.
- **Documents get big.** A real template is hundreds — sometimes
  thousands — of custom elements, and it has to stay responsive.
- **The users aren't software people.** Dentists use this between
  patients, often gloved, always in a hurry.
- **Nothing may be silently mangled.** A note is a legal record; a
  formatting bug is a chart error.

<Callout label="The shape of the thing">
  So the editor has to be a form *and* a document at the same time. You
  interact with it like an app — clicking options, toggling lines on and
  off — and what comes out the other end is clean prose you can paste
  into a twenty-year-old Windows EHR without a single stray character.
</Callout>

### Two documents, one editor

The design that fell out of that: every template is a single **Slate.js**
document that renders in two modes and serialises two ways.

In **use** mode, the clinician clicks through it. Options are buttons.
Lines are inactive by default and only join the note when clicked.
Blanks are inputs. The generated note lives beside the template, updating
as you click, with one Copy button at the bottom.

gutter down the left for which lines are active by default, and a

### The element set

Ten-plus custom Slate element types, all domain-specific, all of them
things a clinician actually needed rather than things a text editor
usually has:

- **Buttons** — inline option groups. Click to select, Ctrl-click for
  multi-select, right-click to *negate* an option (which is how you say
  "no caries" in one gesture instead of a sentence).
- **Clickable lines and clickable text** — content that sits in the
  template greyed out and only enters the note when it's relevant.
- **Blank inputs, single-select dropdowns, multi-select dropdowns,
  grids, toggle blocks, annotations** — the rest of the form vocabulary.
- **A speech-to-text pointer** — an element that marks where dictated
  text is going to land.

### Authoring affordances, and a context menu of our own

Authoring that by hand would be miserable, so the editor grew the
affordances of a real writing tool: a `/` command menu, keyboard
shortcuts for everything, and **Convert** — select any run of text and
turn the elements in it into buttons, or the text in it into buttons,
in one move.

<Figure

That context menu is a good example of how little comes for free here.
Taking over right-click means taking over the clipboard entries and the
history entries with it, so undo and redo run on our own stacks pushed
alongside Slate's — otherwise a converted selection unwinds one element
at a time instead of one action at a time.

### Logic, and elements that outlive a template

Two features took the templates from static to programmable.

**The logic panel** is an if/else system over the custom elements:
*when* this input is `X`, *then* set that dropdown to `Y`. Clinicians
build conditional notes without writing anything resembling code.

<Figure

  caption="WHEN / THEN, over the same elements you just placed in the document."

**Dynamic elements** are defined once and referenced everywhere — your
name, your nurse list, your standard set of materials. Change the
element, and every template that references it changes.

### Dictation that lands in the right place

Transcription is the part everyone asks about, and the interesting
problem isn't the model — it's the seam. Audio comes back as text that
has to be inserted into a live Slate document whose selection may have
moved, inside a template the clinician is still clicking through. The
pointer element exists precisely so the insertion target is explicit
state rather than a guess about where the cursor was a second ago.

The control itself is push-to-talk: hold to speak, drag it anywhere on
screen, slide to lock it open, auto-stop on silence — designed for
someone holding a mirror in their other hand.

### The AI layer, and the rule it has to obey

Three model-backed features sit on top of the note.

- **Summarisation** collapses a long, dictated, circling account of an
  appointment into the tight clinical summary that actually belongs in
  the record.
- **Cleanup** removes the debris dictation leaves behind — false starts,
  duplicated phrases, stray punctuation, sentences that trail off —
  without touching clinical meaning. This is the one where being
  helpful is dangerous. A model that tidies the phrasing of a drug dose
  is a model that has to be fenced in.
- **Auto-format** is Prettier for clinical notes: consistent spacing and
  blank lines between sections, consistent capitalisation of headings
  and abbreviations, and none of the double spaces and orphaned
  punctuation that a half-clicked option leaves behind. Deterministic
  wherever it can be, so the same note always comes out the same shape.

<Callout label="The rule">
  A model returns text. The document is a tree. Dropping a rewritten
  block straight into the selection would flatten every button, dropdown
  and clickable line inside it into dead prose — the template would come
  back looking fine and be permanently broken. So each of these runs
  over a resolved range and writes back as a structured edit, leaving
  the elements it didn't ask to change exactly where they were. And
  nothing lands in the record without the clinician reading it first.
</Callout>

### NotePal: the note leaves the browser

The browser is not where a clinician works. Their patient management
system is the window that matters, and Clerkal spends most of its life
covering it up.

NotePal pops the template out into its own small window that floats on
top of whatever application is in front — the clinician works through
the note against the PMS on screen, then closes it and everything
returns to the main screen with nothing lost.

The interesting part is that it is one editor in two windows, not a
copy. The React tree is rendered into a second window's document, which
means none of the things you take for granted are there any more:
stylesheets don't follow the tree across, events and selection belong to
a different `window`, and the clipboard is that window's clipboard. Get
any of it slightly wrong and you have two editors drifting apart —
which for a clinical note is the worst possible failure mode, because
both copies look plausible.

### Paste is a protocol, not a feature

Clinicians arrive with a decade of notes in Word, Google Docs, Notepad
and rival EHRs, and they paste them in. Each of those sources hands the
clipboard a different, differently-broken flavour of HTML. So paste got
its own parsing layer: normalise the incoming markup per source, map it
onto our element tree, and drop everything that would poison the
document. Copy out runs the same road in reverse, because a note that
pastes into Word with invisible formatting artefacts is a note that
comes back as a support ticket.

### The performance problem, which was the real project

A production template is not a demo. It's hundreds — sometimes
thousands — of custom elements in one document. And the first honest
version of this editor re-rendered nearly all of them on every
keystroke.

The cause was a habit, not a bug. Slate makes editor access easy:
`useSlate()` in any element component and you have the editor. But
`useSlate` subscribes that component to *every* change in the document.
Do it in every element renderer — which is the natural way to write
them — and each character typed invalidates the entire tree. At fifty
elements nobody notices. At two thousand, typing has visible lag, and
clicking a button somewhere in the medical history re-renders the
extraoral exam.

<Illustration
  label="Diagram: before and after, one keystroke"
  note="Left: every element in the tree lit up as re-rendering, because each one subscribed to the editor. Right: the same keystroke lighting up one element."
  ratio="16 / 7"
/>

<Callout label="What actually fixed it">
  `React.memo` on every element renderer, and then the unglamorous
  part — going element by element and replacing editor-hook access with
  explicitly passed props, so a component re-renders when *its own* data
  changes and not when the document does. Memoisation only pays when the
  props are stable, so most of the work was making them stable.
</Callout>

There was no clever trick at the end of it. It was a long stretch of
profiling, changing one element's prop contract, measuring, and moving
to the next — and it taught me more about React's actual rendering model
than any amount of reading had.

The other thing that consumed time and doesn't show up in a commit
graph: the animation and interaction polish. Dentists use this between
patients, sometimes gloved, often in a hurry, and almost none of them
think of themselves as software people. Every transition, hit target and
piece of feedback in the editor got argued over on those terms.

### Everything around the editor

The editor is the hard part but not the whole product. Alongside it:

- **Templates, folders and a shared library**, scoped by country and
  language, so a UK dental practice sees UK dental templates.
- **Teams and clinics** — sharing templates and documents across a
  practice, with clinic-level branding on patient-facing letters.
- **Role-based access control** over all of it: who can edit a shared
  template versus who can only use it, and what a clinic owner can do
  that a member can't.
- **Stripe subscriptions**, including group billing for clinics.
- **Redux Toolkit and RTK Query** for global state and the API layer,
  over a **Node.js / Express / MongoDB** backend that stores the
  document tree in the shape the editor already speaks.

## 4 — Outcome

Clerkal is live and in daily clinical use. A note that used to be typed
out in full is now clicked together from a template the practice
maintains itself, dictated into where it needs prose, tidied by the AI
layer, and copied into the patient record in one action. The company's
own pitch is that it cuts record-keeping by up to 70% — having watched
how much of a note is the same note every time, that isn't the
surprising part.

What it took to get there, on the editor side:

- **A document model that holds up.** Ten-plus custom element types,
  each a stable shape that survives saving, sharing, duplicating and
  pasting between templates.
- **Performance at real scale.** Templates of thousands of elements that
  type without lag, after the re-render work above.
- **The seams that make the model useful.** Dictation, summarisation,
  cleanup and formatting that write back into the tree as structured
  edits rather than flattening it into dead text.
- **The product around it.** Templates, library, teams, clinics, RBAC,
  Stripe subscriptions and group billing.

### What I'd do differently

I'd version the element schema from the first commit. The element types
multiplied fast, and every one of them is a shape that lives in real
users' saved documents forever — migrating them later is much more
delicate than getting the shape right early.

I'd also build the logic engine on a typed AST from day one rather than
growing it out of the panel UI, and I'd write the performance rule down
as a lint rule instead of a habit: an element renderer may not subscribe
to the editor. That one line, enforced, would have saved weeks.

### What I took from it

Most of what I learned here wasn't Slate — it was React's actual
rendering model, learned the hard way with a profiler open, and the
discipline of treating a document as data with a contract rather than as
markup. The other half is domain: when the user is a clinician between
patients, the measure of a feature isn't what it can do, it's how few
gestures it takes and how obviously it behaves.
